A Supercomputer in Disguise

"The real voyage of discovery consists not in seeking new landscapes, but in having new eyes." — Marcel Proust

We are often conditioned to believe that finding what we are looking for requires venturing into the unknown. We travel to new cities for inspiration, change environments to find happiness and constantly search outside of ourselves to find answers, deeply convinced that whatever is missing from our lives must be far away. We equate distance with discovery, completely forgetting that we don't always have to go looking. We constantly ignore the possibility that what we are searching for is already resting within. It is a strange flaw in human nature that the longer we look at something, the less we actually see it. Familiarity breeds a kind of blindness. We assume that because something has always been used one way, that is the only way it can ever exist.

True discovery comes when we take what we already have, look at it from another angle and completely reimagine its purpose.

In 2006, this exact shift in perspective hit Nvidia when their engineers realised something profound.

The parallel processing architecture that allowed their GPU to calculate thousands of pixels simultaneously was capable of far more than video games. If it was programmable, it could calculate anything. It was actually a supercomputer in disguise.

To prove it, Nvidia took a gamble and released CUDA, a proprietary software interface that unlocked their GPUs for general purpose computing. This turned out to be the ultimate Trojan horse. Overnight, the scientific world shifted. Researchers working on impossibly complex and time consuming problems could suddenly do their work more than ten times faster by simply buying an Nvidia gaming card, then tuning it with the CUDA software, and adding it as an extension to their PC, effectively making it a supercomputer. Recognising the magnitude of this shift, Jensen embarked on a global campaign to hunt down every single artificial intelligence researcher on the planet. He didn't just sell the hardware and software to them; he befriended them.

By personally feeding CUDA and his GPUs to the world's greatest minds, and obsessively tracking their feedback, Nvidia captured the Artificial Intelligence Revolution from the very start, creating an inescapable walled garden around the future of computing.

This obsessive networking paid off in 2012 when a neural network called AlexNet was entered into the world's toughest image-recognition competition.

AlexNet broke all previous benchmarks by an impossible margin just because it was trained on two off the shelf Nvidia GTX 580 gaming GPUs instead of traditional CPUs.

This made Jensen realise that deep learning was capable of solving virtually any problem at scale. So they didn't wait for the rest of the industry to catch on. Instead, they quietly poured billions into engineering a machine for a market that financially did not even exist.

In 2016, Jensen walked onto a stage and unveiled the DGX-1 which was designed to be an AI supercomputer crammed into a single metal box, which was priced at $300,000.

Upon the unveiling, the reaction of the crowd was actually dead silence, simply because no one understood what it was and the sheer capability it held. To an average analyst, it looked as though Nvidia had simply set billions on fire by building an overpriced server for a niche experiment.

However, watching from the corners, Elon Musk grasped the sheer gravity of what Jensen had just unleashed. He recognised the DGX-1 as the exact machine his new nonprofit would need to break the limits of computing.

A few weeks later, Jensen drove to San Francisco and signed the world's first production unit of the DGX-1 and hand delivered it to Musk and the founding team of OpenAI, who would later become the creators of ChatGPT. Nvidia had quietly lit the match that would start the global AI wildfire.

Today that wildfire is consuming the entire tech industry, and Nvidia is the only one selling water. Giants like Google, Microsoft and Apple are pouring billions into artificial intelligence; but every time a competitor wants to train a new model, they have to pay the toll to Nvidia's hardware and CUDA software ecosystem.

A company that was once thirty days from bankruptcy didn't just survive the future, they went on to predict the AI revolution and engineer the exact tracks it runs on today.

It was a silent, decades long checkmate that the rest of the world never saw coming.
